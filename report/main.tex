\title{
When Does Adaptive Coordinate Sampling Pay for Itself?\\
\large Ridge-Regression Baselines, ASCD, and Oracle-Quality Analysis
}
\author{Ng Jing Yi, Clara}
\date{September 2026}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We study coordinate-selection rules for ridge regression under feature
scaling and correlation. A baseline experiment compares uniform sampling,
fixed Lipschitz sampling, periodically refreshed gradient-gain sampling,
and a progress-triggered gradient-gain heuristic across four synthetic
regimes and the packaged diabetes regression dataset. Each method is
evaluated over 12 seeded runs using a common coordinate update and a
2,400-update budget.

We subsequently implement Approximate Steepest Coordinate Descent (ASCD)
of Stich, Raj, and Jaggi using the paper's zero oracle with exact
full-gradient initialization. On the scaled-correlated case, this
configuration reaches the target in 9 of 12 runs compared with 6 of 12
for uniform sampling, but records greater proxy work on all five seeds
where both methods succeed.

A further oracle-quality analysis explains why the zero-oracle ASCD active
set remains effectively full. On the synthetic cases the full active set
is used on approximately 99.94\% of updates, and the median fraction of
coordinates with zero certified lower bound is one. An exact inner-product
oracle reduces the median active set to one coordinate and reaches the
target in all runs. After charging a modelled dense-Gram setup cost, the
exact oracle is cheaper than the zero oracle on the correlated cases and
diabetes but more expensive on the two independent synthetic cases.
Simulated approximate oracles at the tested intermediate precisions do not
provide a useful middle regime. These experiments are comparisons on our
frozen benchmark rather than reproductions of the paper's published
figures, and no claim of general superiority or algorithmic novelty is
made.
\end{abstract}


\section{Motivation and related work}

Randomized updates and adaptive information collection feature in
stochastic block BFGS \cite{gower2016}, minibatch semi-stochastic
optimization \cite{konecny2016}, parallel and accelerated coordinate
descent \cite{richtarik2016,fercoq2015}, and randomized linear solvers
\cite{gower2015}. Adaptive coordinate-selection methods include adaptive
probabilities for stochastic dual coordinate ascent \cite{csiba2015},
adaptive importance sampling \cite{perekrestenko2017}, and Approximate
Steepest Coordinate Descent (ASCD) \cite{stich2017}.

These works prevent gradient-informed sampling or refreshes alone from
being treated as a new idea. The practical question considered here is
instead when additional coordinate-selection information improves
optimization enough to compensate for the work required to obtain and
maintain it.

The study proceeds in three stages. First, four baseline selection rules
are compared. Second, a published ASCD configuration using the paper's
zero oracle is implemented on the same frozen benchmark. Third, the ASCD
active-set mechanism is diagnosed and compared across zero, approximate,
and exact oracle-quality configurations.


\section{Experimental setup and baseline methods}

For a design matrix $X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ and response $y$, we
minimize
\begin{equation}
f(w)=
\frac{1}{2}\lVert Xw-y\rVert_2^2+
\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert w\rVert_2^2,
\qquad
\lambda=0.03.
\end{equation}

With residual $r=Xw-y$,
\begin{equation}
g_j=X_j^{\mathsf T}r+\lambda w_j,
\qquad
L_j=\lVert X_j\rVert_2^2+\lambda,
\end{equation}
and each baseline method applies
\begin{equation}
w_j \leftarrow w_j-\frac{g_j}{L_j}
\end{equation}
and updates the residual.

Uniform sampling uses $p_j=1/d$, whereas fixed Lipschitz sampling uses
\[
p_j=\frac{L_j}{\sum_k L_k}.
\]
Both gradient-gain variants use
\begin{equation}
p_j=
\frac{0.05}{d}
+
0.95
\frac{g_j^2/L_j}{\sum_k g_k^2/L_k},
\end{equation}
with uniform fallback when the denominator vanishes.

Scheduled gain refreshes initially and every 60 updates. Triggered gain
refreshes at step zero and subsequently at a 120-step checkpoint if the
latest objective decrease is below half the preceding decrease, subject
to a 60-step minimum interval.

The four synthetic cases cross balanced versus geometrically scaled
features with independent versus eight-group correlated features, with
$n=400$ and $d=48$. The packaged diabetes design provides a fifth case.

Each method uses 12 problem seeds and at most 2,400 coordinate updates.
A dense ridge solve defines $f^\ast$ for evaluation. A run succeeds if a
saved checkpoint satisfies
\[
f(w)-f^\ast \leq 10^{-4}.
\]

The reference optimum is used only for evaluation and not by the
selection rules.

\section{Published ASCD comparison}

\subsection{Selection rule}

ASCD \cite{stich2017} maintains, for each coordinate $j$, an estimated
gradient $\widetilde g_j$ and certified radius $r_j$ satisfying
\[
|g_j-\widetilde g_j|\leq r_j.
\]

The corresponding upper and lower bounds are
\begin{equation}
u_j=|\widetilde g_j|+r_j,
\qquad
\ell_j=\max\{0,|\widetilde g_j|-r_j\}.
\end{equation}

The active set is the smallest nonempty subset $I$ such that every
excluded coordinate satisfies
\begin{equation}
u_j^2
<
\frac{1}{|I|}
\sum_{i\in I}\ell_i^2.
\end{equation}

Equality therefore does not permit exclusion. A coordinate is drawn
uniformly from members of $I$ attaining the largest lower bound.

Our implementation initializes $\widetilde g$ with the exact full gradient
and all radii at zero.


\subsection{Zero oracle}

For the Stage 3 comparison, the paper's zero cross-coordinate oracle is
used. If coordinate $i$ changes by $h$, then for $j\neq i$
\begin{equation}
g_j^{\mathrm{new}}-g_j
=
hX_j^{\mathsf T}X_i.
\end{equation}

Cauchy--Schwarz gives
\begin{equation}
|g_j^{\mathrm{new}}-g_j|
\leq
|h|\,\lVert X_j\rVert_2\,\lVert X_i\rVert_2.
\end{equation}

The stored cross-gradient estimate is therefore unchanged and its
certified radius is increased by this bound.

For the selected coordinate,
\[
h=-\frac{g_i}{L_i},
\]
and exact minimization gives
\[
g_i^{\mathrm{new}}=g_i+hL_i=0.
\]

The selected estimate and radius can therefore be reset to zero up to
numerical precision.

\section{Stage 4: oracle-quality mechanism analysis}

\subsection{Zero-oracle diagnosis}

The Stage 3 median active set alone does not reveal whether the active set
is usually large or merely has a large median. Stage 4 therefore replays
the frozen zero-oracle trajectory while recording per-update bound and
active-set diagnostics.

The replay validates every saved Stage 3 checkpoint and reproduces the
frozen objective gaps and proxy work.

For the four synthetic cases, the full 48-coordinate active set is used
on approximately 99.94\% of recorded updates. On diabetes, the full
10-coordinate set is used on approximately 99.96\% of updates. The
median fraction of coordinates with zero lower bound is 1.0000 for every
case.

Since
\[
\ell_j=\max\{0,|\widetilde g_j|-r_j\},
\]
a zero lower bound occurs when the certified radius dominates the stored
estimate. If all lower bounds are zero, then
\[
\frac{1}{|I|}
\sum_{i\in I}\ell_i^2=0,
\]
so no coordinate can satisfy the strict safe-exclusion condition
$u_j^2<0$.

The diagnostic radius trajectories are consistent with this mechanism:
the certified radius generally remains above the typical true-gradient
scale.


\subsection{Oracle-quality experiment}

Phase B retains the frozen benchmark and coordinate update while changing
the oracle information.

We compare:

\begin{itemize}
\item the zero oracle $g^3$;
\item simulated approximate oracles $g^2$ with
$\epsilon\in\{1,0.5,0.25,0.125\}$;
\item the exact inner-product oracle $g^1$.
\end{itemize}

The simulated $g^2$ runs isolate oracle-quality effects only. They do not
implement or cost a practical embedding or approximation-acquisition
mechanism.

The $g^3$ configuration reproduces the frozen Stage 3 trajectory.

\subsection{Active-set transition}

The exact oracle produces a qualitatively different selection regime.

For $g^1$, the median active set is one coordinate on every case, and
the 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th, and 90th active-set percentiles are all one.
In contrast, $g^3$ and simulated $g^2$ with $\epsilon=1$ retain the full
coordinate dimension as their median active set.

The intermediate simulated $g^2$ configurations technically prune on
almost every update, but only weakly: their synthetic median active set
is 47 of 48 coordinates, and the diabetes median is 9 of 10 coordinates.

Thus the tested approximate precision levels do not provide a useful
middle regime between the zero and exact oracles.

\subsection{Optimization behavior}

The objective-gap trajectories show the same qualitative separation.

Exact $g^1$ reaches the target in every run and converges substantially
faster in coordinate updates. The simulated $\epsilon=1$ configuration
largely resembles the zero oracle. The intermediate simulated
configurations with $\epsilon\in\{0.5,0.25,0.125\}$ do not reach the target
in any run.

This non-monotonic behavior should be interpreted narrowly. Changing the
approximate oracle changes both the maintained gradient estimates and the
coordinate-selection trajectory. The experiment therefore does not show
that tighter approximate oracles are generally harmful; it shows that the
tested simulated configurations do not produce a successful intermediate
regime under this benchmark.

\subsection{Does exact oracle information pay for itself?}

The common ASCD core proxy does not include the cost of obtaining an exact
Gram matrix. For $g^1$, Stage 4 therefore adds a separate dense setup
charge
\[
nd^2.
\]

For the synthetic cases this is
\[
400\cdot48^2=921{,}600,
\]
and for diabetes it is
\[
442\cdot10^2=44{,}200.
\]

\section{Interpretation and limitations}

The baseline experiment shows that the simple gradient-gain heuristics do
not succeed consistently across the frozen cases.

The initial zero-oracle ASCD comparison improves scaled-correlated success
count under a fixed update budget, but does not show lower recorded work
on shared successful seeds.

Stage 4 explains a major part of this behavior. Under the zero oracle,
certified uncertainty overwhelms the stored estimates sufficiently often
that almost every lower bound becomes zero. The safe active set is
therefore effectively full.

Exact oracle information reverses this mechanism completely and produces
an active set of one coordinate. This improves optimization strongly, but
the cost of obtaining the exact information determines whether the
overall proxy improves.

The result is regime-dependent rather than universal.

The simulated approximate-oracle experiment has an important limitation:
it measures oracle quality without implementing a realistic acquisition
mechanism. Its results therefore cannot answer whether a practical
approximate inner-product oracle would pay for itself.

The tested intermediate precision settings also produce an unusual
non-monotonic optimization pattern. They should be reported as a result
of this particular simulation and benchmark rather than generalized.

Only one small packaged real dataset is included. No predictive
generalization performance is evaluated. The proxy is not an exact FLOP
count, and wall-clock measurements depend on software, hardware, and setup
boundaries.

Finally, the ASCD paper's one-step comparison assumes common smoothness
and update conditions that are not identical to this benchmark, which
uses coordinate-specific $L_j$ values and its existing exact
coordinate-minimization rule. The paper's theoretical result should
therefore not be interpreted as a guarantee for the experiments reported
here.


\section{Conclusion and future research}

The study now provides a progression from exploratory baseline sampling
rules to a published ASCD mechanism and then to a direct analysis of
oracle quality.

The zero-oracle ASCD active set remains almost always full because its
certified lower bounds collapse to zero. Exact inner-product information
removes this mechanism, reduces the median active set to one coordinate,
and reaches the target in every run.

Whether the extra information pays for itself depends on the benchmark
regime. Under the modelled dense-Gram setup charge, exact $g^1$ is more
expensive than $g^3$ on the independent synthetic cases but cheaper on
the correlated cases and diabetes.

The tested simulated approximate oracles do not provide a successful
middle regime. Under the predeclared Stage 4 decision rule, these results
do not justify proceeding directly to a costed implementation of the
tested approximate precision levels.

A finer precision sweep between $\epsilon=0.125$ and the exact limit could
be investigated later, but would be a post-hoc exploratory extension.
Likewise, a genuinely costed approximate-inner-product implementation
would require its acquisition mechanism to be specified and charged
explicitly before making cost-effectiveness claims.

Broader datasets, repeated wall-clock measurements, uncertainty analyses,
and harmonized implementation boundaries would be required before making
stronger claims about general computational advantage.
